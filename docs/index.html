Which Draft Persuades?

Two versions of an argument in; a calibrated verdict on which one is more likely to change the reader's mind, and what would make the weaker one stronger. Built for replies in a debate or discussion (it learned from Reddit's ChangeMyView); it warns when a draft looks unlike that, such as marketing copy.

It answers: which of two drafts answering the same view is more likely to change the reader's mind, what separates them, and how far to trust that number.

How the verdict is decided the model, its training data, its calibration

A paired model, because persuasion is relative. The model never scores a draft on its own. It was trained on pairs of replies to the same ChangeMyView post where one reply changed the poster's mind (they awarded a delta) and the other did not, the design of Tan, Niculae, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil & Lee, Winning Arguments (WWW 2016). Comparing two answers to the same view controls the topic, which is what makes the comparison meaningful.

What it reads. Twelve plain features per draft (length, how much it covers the poster's points, whether it argues in its own words or echoes theirs, direct address, questions, examples, numbers, hedging, links, acknowledgement, sentence length), listed with the learned weights in "The full numbers". A logistic regression on the difference between the two drafts' features gives a probability, recalibrated on held-out data (Platt scaling) so that "70%" means what it says as closely as the data allows.

Training and the frozen split. 4,263 pairs from the authors' Winning Arguments corpus (r/ChangeMyView, 2013–2015), split by conversation using the corpus's own train/holdout flag: 2,772 pairs fitted the model, 684 calibrated it, and 807 were held out and touched once, for the numbers on this page. The plan was frozen before training (PLAN.md in the repository).

Calibration on the held-out pairs

Read it as: when the model puts a draft in the 70–80% band, that draft actually won about as often as the band says. Bands at the extremes hold few pairs, so they are noisier. Overall the predictions are off by about 4 points on average (expected calibration error 0.035).

Limits. The model learned from one community's arguments, in 2013–2015, in one format (a written reply to a stated view). It reads surface features only: it cannot tell whether a claim is true, and a draft can game it. Treat the verdict as a second reader with known blind spots, not as a judge.

The training write-up, weights and per-pair held-out predictions are in the repository's Research notes.

When to use it, and when not assumptions, limits, alternatives

Use it to choose between two versions of a reply in a discussion or debate (answering someone's stated view, a review comment, a forum post or an op-ed rebuttal) and to see which surface features separate them.

It assumes the following. Each line says what happens to the answer when your case breaks the assumption.

Do not use it to judge marketing copy or ads (test them on real readers; the A/B Test Decision Tool reads the result), to fact-check a claim, to score a single draft on its own, or for decisions where being wrong a third of the time is too costly.

Where this tool comes from the problem and data it was built on, and how it was generalised

The tool is a trained model, and the study is the model. It follows Tan, Niculae, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil and Lee (2016), Winning Arguments, who collected replies on Reddit's ChangeMyView, where a poster awards a "delta" to the reply that changed their mind. From that corpus, 4,263 pairs were built: two replies to the same post, one of which earned the delta.

The plan was frozen before training (split, twelve interpretable features, a logistic model, the metrics). The model was then fitted on 2,772 pairs, its probabilities calibrated on 684 others, and tested once on 807 held-out pairs: right on 65.6%, with predicted bands tracking actual win rates (average error about 4 points). Length was the strongest single signal; replies that echoed the poster's exact words did worse.

How it was generalised. The model scores any two drafts that answer the same view, not only Reddit replies. To keep that honest, the tool adds what the study did not need: a domain warning for text unlike the training data, an equal-length score so length alone cannot decide the verdict, and an optional AI edit whose result is re-scored by the same model. A comparison with a language-model judge is planned and not yet run.
